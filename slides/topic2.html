Demand

How do consumers respond to price — and what makes them want more or less of a good?

2
Demand

The Law of Demand

When price rises, quantity demanded falls — all else equal (ceteris paribus).

💸 Income Effect

Higher price → lower real purchasing power → consumers buy less

🔄 Substitution Effect

Higher price → the good is relatively more expensive → switch to cheaper alternatives

Demand is a relationship between price and quantity demanded — not a single number.

A demand schedule lists Qd at each price. A demand curve graphs it — always downward sloping.

3
Demand

The Demand Curve

Lower price → more units demanded

At any price on the y-axis, the demand curve tells you how many units consumers want to buy.

  • Always downward sloping — P and Qd move in opposite directions
  • A movement along the curve = change in price, ceteris paribus
  • A shift of the entire curve = change in something other than price
4
Demand

Movement Along vs. Shift of the Demand Curve

Shift = whole curve moves · Movement = slide along D₁

📌 Movement along the curve

Caused only by a change in the good's own price. Everything else is held constant (ceteris paribus).

↔ Shift of the curve

Caused by a change in any determinant of demand other than price — income, tastes, other-good prices, etc.

Demand increases → curve shifts right  |  Demand decreases → curve shifts left

5
Demand

What Shifts the Demand Curve?

💰

Income

Normal goods: ↑ income → ↑ demand.
Inferior goods: ↑ income → ↓ demand.

🏦

Wealth

Greater wealth → more spending power → demand increases for most goods.

🔄

Prices of Related Goods

Substitutes: ↑ PPepsi → ↑ demand for Coke.
Complements: ↑ Pgas → ↓ demand for SUVs.

❤️

Tastes & Preferences

Trends, advertising, and cultural shifts change what consumers want — even without price changes.

🔮

Expectations

Expecting prices to rise soon → buy more today. Expected future income also matters.

👥

Number of Buyers

More consumers in the market → larger total demand at every price level.

6
Demand

Normal vs. Inferior Goods

Normal Good

Demand increases when income rises.

Restaurant meals, new cars, electronics, vacations, brand-name clothing

↑ Income  →  D shifts right

Inferior Good

Demand decreases when income rises — consumers trade up to better alternatives.

Bus rides, instant ramen, store-brand goods, used clothing

↑ Income  →  D shifts left

Whether a good is normal or inferior depends on individual preference — there is no physical property that determines it.

Supply

How do producers respond to price — and what determines how much they're willing to sell?

8
Supply

The Law of Supply

When price rises, quantity supplied rises — ceteris paribus.

💡 Why does supply slope upward?

Higher prices make production more profitable. Existing firms produce more; new firms enter the market.

🍕 Pizza Example

At $5/pizza the firm earns $0 profit. At $10/pizza it earns $5 each. Higher price → more worth producing.

Supply is a relationship between price and quantity supplied.

A supply schedule lists Qs at each price. A supply curve graphs it — always upward sloping.

9
Supply

The Supply Curve

Higher price → more units supplied

At any price, the supply curve tells you how much producers are willing and able to sell.

  • Always upward sloping — P and Qs move in the same direction
  • Movement along the curve = change in price
  • Shift of curve = change in a non-price determinant of supply
10
Supply

What Shifts the Supply Curve?

🏭

Cost of Inputs

↑ wages, raw materials, or energy → production costs rise → supply decreases (shifts left)

⚙️

Technology

Better technology → lower cost per unit → supply increases (shifts right)

🔮

Expectations

If sellers expect higher future prices, they may withhold supply today to sell at higher prices later.

🏢

Number of Sellers

More firms enter the market → total market supply increases at every price level.

Supply increases → curve shifts right  |  Supply decreases → curve shifts left

Chapter 4
⚖️

Market Equilibrium

Where demand and supply meet — and what happens when they don't.

12
Market Equilibrium

Market-Clearing Equilibrium

At P*, quantity demanded = quantity supplied

⚖️ Equilibrium Defined

The price P* at which Qd = Qs. The market clears — no unsold goods, no unmet demand.

  • No pressure on price to change at P*
  • Every buyer willing to pay P* can buy; every seller willing to sell at P* can sell
  • The market is "cleared"
13
Market Equilibrium

When Price Is Too Low: Shortage

At P_low: Qd > Qs → shortage

Price below P* → Qd > Qs

  • Consumers want more than firms will supply at that price
  • Unsatisfied buyers bid the price up
  • Price rises toward P* — shortage self-corrects
🏠 Example

Rent control: price ceiling below market rent → chronic housing shortage

14
Market Equilibrium

When Price Is Too High: Surplus

At P_high: Qs > Qd → surplus

Price above P* → Qs > Qd

  • Firms produce more than consumers want to buy
  • Unsold inventory → sellers cut the price
  • Price falls toward P* — surplus self-corrects
🌾 Example

Agricultural price floors: support prices above equilibrium → chronic crop surpluses and government stockpiles

15
Market Equilibrium

Markets Are Self-Correcting

Price is a signal. It automatically pushes the market back toward equilibrium.

📉

Shortage → Price Rises

Unsatisfied demand bids price up
→ Qs rises, Qd falls
→ gap closes
→ equilibrium restored

⚖️
📦

Surplus → Price Falls

Unsold inventory pushes price down
→ Qd rises, Qs falls
→ gap closes
→ equilibrium restored

In competitive markets, no central planner is needed — the price mechanism coordinates buyers and sellers automatically.

16
Comparative Statics

Supply Increases → New Equilibrium

S shifts right → P falls · Q rises

🍓 Strawberry Harvest Example

A bumper harvest → supply increases → more berries available at every price

  • S shifts right → new E at lower P, higher Q
  • Consumers benefit — price falls
  • More units traded in the market overall

Same result from: better technology · lower input costs · more sellers entering the market

17
Comparative Statics

Demand Increases → New Equilibrium

D shifts right → P rises · Q rises

✈️ Airline Tickets Example

Post-pandemic travel rebound → more people flying → demand for tickets surges

  • D shifts right → new E at higher P, higher Q
  • Producers benefit — price rises
  • More units traded in the market overall

Same result from: ↑ income · ↑ population · shift in tastes · ↑ price of a substitute

18
ECON 2006 · Topic 2 Summary

Key Takeaways

D

Law of Demand: P ↑ → Qd ↓ (downward-sloping curve). Curve shifts right (increase) or left (decrease) when non-price determinants change: income, wealth, related-good prices, tastes, expectations, or number of buyers.

S

Law of Supply: P ↑ → Qs ↑ (upward-sloping curve). Curve shifts right (increase) when input costs fall, technology improves, more sellers enter, or sellers expect lower future prices.

E

Equilibrium: Qd = Qs at price P*. Shortages push price up; surpluses push price down. Comparative statics: trace any shift to find the new P* and Q* — always check direction of both.

Markets self-correct through the price mechanism — no central coordination required.
