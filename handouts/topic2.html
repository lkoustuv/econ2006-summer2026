1   Demand

1.1 What Is Demand?

When economists talk about demand, they mean something more precise than the everyday use of the word. Demand is not simply wanting something — it is wanting it and being able to pay for it and being observed at a specific price. These distinctions matter enormously for analysis.

Core Definition
Quantity Demanded

The amount (number of units) of a product that a household would buy in a given time period if it could buy all it wanted at the current market price.

Three phrases in that definition deserve special attention:

  • At the market price. Individual buyers are price-takers — they cannot set the price themselves. They simply decide how much to buy at whatever price the market offers.
  • In a given time period. Quantity demanded is only meaningful relative to a time window. Saying someone demands “five burgers” is ambiguous; saying “five burgers per week” is not. The time qualifier can change the number dramatically — five burgers per week versus five burgers per year are very different quantities.
  • If it could buy all it wanted. For demand to be meaningful, willingness to buy and ability to pay must both be present. Wanting a Ferrari doesn’t constitute demand unless you can also pay for it.

The full demand for a good is the entire relationship between price and quantity demanded — not just the quantity at one particular price. We summarize this relationship with a demand schedule (table) and a demand curve (graph).

1.2 The Law of Demand

Law of Economics
The Law of Demand

There is a negative (inverse) relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded, all else equal (ceteris paribus). As price rises, quantity demanded falls; as price falls, quantity demanded rises.

Why does this hold? Two complementary effects explain it:

  • Substitution effect. When a good becomes more expensive, consumers switch to cheaper alternatives that satisfy a similar need. If coffee prices spike, more people drink tea.
  • Income effect. A price increase effectively reduces the buyer’s real purchasing power. The same nominal income now buys less, so consumers cut back on their consumption of the now-pricier good.

The Law of Demand produces a downward-sloping demand curve — one of the most robust empirical regularities in all of economics.

⚠ Apparent Exceptions
When Demand Curves Might Not Slope Downward

Necessities with no substitutes (e.g., insulin): Diabetics must take insulin regardless of price. The quantity demanded barely changes as price rises, making the curve nearly vertical. This is extreme price inelasticity — not a violation of the law of demand, just a very steep curve.

Giffen goods: Theoretically, for a very inferior good that is a large share of a poor household’s budget, a price increase could reduce real income so severely that the household buys more of it because it can no longer afford better substitutes. These are rare in practice. Luxury status goods (“Veblen goods”) can also display perverse demand behavior, but for different reasons (higher price signals prestige).

1.4 What Shifts the Demand Curve? The Six Determinants

The demand curve is drawn holding everything constant except price. When any of the other underlying factors change, the entire curve shifts — reflecting a change in demand at every price level. There are six key determinants.

  (a) Income

Income affects how much people can afford to spend. But the direction of the effect depends on the type of good:

  • Normal goods — demand rises when income rises (and falls when income falls). Most goods are normal. If your income increases, you buy more steak, take more vacations, purchase a bigger apartment. Income ↑ → Demand curve shifts right.
  • Inferior goods — demand falls when income rises. These are goods consumers switch away from as they become wealthier. Classic examples: instant ramen, generic-brand cereal, bus rides. If your income doubles, you might stop eating ramen every night and eat at restaurants instead. Income ↑ → Demand curve shifts left.
Key Distinction: “Inferior” is an economic classification, not a judgment about quality. An inferior good is simply one for which demand and income move in opposite directions.

  (b) Wealth

Wealth and income are related but distinct. Income is a flow — money received per period (salary, wages). Wealth is a stock — the accumulated value of assets (home equity, savings, stocks). Someone can have high wealth but low current income (a retiree living off savings) or high income but little wealth (a new professional with student loans). In general, higher wealth increases demand for normal goods, much like income does. The U.S. housing wealth boom of the early 2000s — and the resulting consumption surge — is a dramatic historical example.

  (c) Prices of Related Goods

The demand for a good is affected by the prices of other goods in two distinct ways, depending on the relationship between them:

  • Substitutes are goods that serve similar functions and can replace each other. Coffee and tea; Coke and Pepsi; butter and margarine; Uber and Lyft. If the price of a substitute rises, consumers switch to the original good, shifting its demand curve right. If the price of a substitute falls, they switch away, shifting demand left.
    Rule: Price of substitute ↑ → Demand for good shifts right.
  • Complements are goods consumed together — they go hand in hand. Hot dogs and buns; cars and gasoline; printers and ink; streaming services and smart TVs. If the price of a complement rises, consumption of both typically falls, shifting demand for the original good left. If the price of a complement falls, demand for the original good rises.
    Rule: Price of complement ↑ → Demand for good shifts left.
Worked Example 1.1
Coke vs. Pepsi (Substitutes) and Hot Dogs vs. Buns (Complements)

Situation A: Coca-Cola raises its price from $1.50 to $2.50 per can.

Since Coke and Pepsi are substitutes, Coke’s price increase causes consumers to switch to Pepsi. The demand curve for Pepsi shifts rightward: at every price, more Pepsi is now demanded than before.

Situation B: A spike in charcoal prices doubles the cost of grilling for summer cookouts.

Charcoal and hot dogs are complements — people use them together. A higher charcoal price discourages grilling, so fewer hot dogs are demanded. The demand curve for hot dogs shifts leftward.

  (d) Tastes and Preferences

Consumer preferences are shaped by culture, advertising, social trends, health information, and fads. A positive shift in preferences toward a good increases demand; a negative shift decreases it. The hula hoop craze of 1958 is a textbook example: over 100 million units were sold within a year, purely because of a consumer fad with no change in price or income.

Advertising attempts to shift demand curves rightward by making goods seem more desirable. Public health campaigns can shift demand for cigarettes leftward. A viral social media trend can boost demand for a product overnight. All of these operate on the tastes-and-preferences channel.

  (e) Future Expectations

Consumers act not just on current prices and incomes but on what they expect in the future. Two kinds of expectations matter:

  • Expected future price: If consumers expect a good’s price to rise in the future, many will buy more now — shifting current demand right. Conversely, if they expect prices to fall (say, waiting for the new model year to arrive), current demand shifts left. Car dealerships in late summer — when new model-year vehicles arrive and older models are discounted — experience a spike in demand as buyers rush to get end-of-year deals.
  • Expected future income: A law student in their final year, expecting a high salary upon graduation, may increase current spending on credit even before earning more.

  (f) Number of Buyers in the Market

An increase in the number of buyers (population growth, new market entrants, relaxed trade barriers allowing foreign buyers) shifts market demand rightward. A decrease in buyers shifts it leftward.

1.5 Movement Along the Curve vs. Shift of the Curve

This is one of the most important distinctions in all of economics, and one of the most common sources of error. You must never confuse the two:

  • A change in the price of the good itself → causes a movement along the existing demand curve. We call this a “change in quantity demanded.” The curve does not move; you move to a different point on it.
  • A change in any non-price determinant (income, related prices, tastes, expectations, number of buyers) → causes the entire curve to shift. We call this a “change in demand.” A new curve is drawn.
Jargon Rule: Always say “change in quantity demanded” when price changes (movement along). Say “change in demand” when a non-price factor changes (shift of curve). Using “demand increased” to describe a price-induced change is a technical error.
Figure 1.2 — Movement Along vs. Shift of the Demand Curve
A price change moves you along D₁ (blue arrows A→B). A non-price change shifts the entire curve rightward to D₂ (increase in demand) or leftward to D₀ (decrease in demand).

1.6 From Individual Demand to Market Demand

An individual demand curve shows one household’s demand. Market demand is the total demand of all buyers in the market. It is obtained by horizontal summation: at every price, we add up the quantities demanded by all individual buyers.

If Aisha demands 3 pairs of shoes and Ben demands 2 pairs at $60, market demand at $60 is 5 pairs. Do this for every price and you get the market demand curve. Because there are more buyers in the market than any single household, the market demand curve lies further to the right — but it still slopes downward.

2   Supply

2.1 What Is Supply?

Core Definition
Supply

The amount of a good that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at a given price, during a given time period. As with demand, both willingness and ability must be present.

The key actors on the supply side are firms — businesses that transform inputs (labor, raw materials, capital) into outputs (goods and services) in pursuit of profit. The amount they choose to produce and sell depends on several factors, foremost among them the price they can charge.

2.2 The Law of Supply

Law of Economics
The Law of Supply

There is a positive relationship between the price of a good and the quantity supplied, ceteris paribus. Higher prices encourage more production; lower prices lead to less.

Why does supply slope upward? The answer comes from how profit-maximizing firms respond to price changes.

2.4 What Shifts the Supply Curve? The Key Determinants

Like demand, the supply curve is drawn holding non-price factors constant. When those factors change, the entire curve shifts.

  (a) Input / Production Costs

Profit = Revenue − Cost. Anything that raises production costs reduces profit at every output level, making firms less willing to produce. Higher costs shift the supply curve leftward — meaning less is supplied at every price. This includes wages, raw material prices, energy costs, and regulatory compliance expenses.

A decrease in costs — say, from cheaper imported materials or falling energy prices — has the opposite effect: supply shifts rightward, meaning more is offered at every price.

Cost ↑ → Supply shifts left. Cost ↓ → Supply shifts right.

🌍 Real-World Connection
How Rising Cocoa Prices Hit Chocolate Makers

In 2023–2024, a severe drought in West Africa devastated cocoa crops, pushing cocoa prices to record highs (over $10,000 per metric ton by early 2024). Cocoa is a key input for chocolate manufacturers. As their input cost soared, their supply curve for chocolate products shifted leftward: at any given retail price, they could profitably produce fewer chocolate bars. The result: chocolate prices rose sharply, and some producers shrank package sizes rather than raise sticker prices — a phenomenon economists call “shrinkflation.”

  (b) Technology

Technological progress allows firms to produce more output from the same inputs — which is economically equivalent to a reduction in costs. Better assembly-line methods, automation, more efficient software, or improved logistics all shift supply rightward: more can be produced at any given price.

Technology rarely shifts supply leftward (technology advances don’t reverse), but a sudden loss of access to technology — say, sanctions cutting off access to specialized equipment — could.

  (c) Expectations About Future Prices

Firms also respond to what they expect to happen, not just what is happening now. If a firm expects the price of its product to be much higher next month, it may withhold current supply — store inventory now to sell later at higher prices. This reduces current supply (shifts left). If a future price collapse is expected, the firm may rush to sell now, temporarily boosting current supply (shifts right).

This is exactly why oil-producing countries sometimes respond to announced future production cuts by pumping more oil today, before the price impact hits.

  (d) Number of Sellers / New Entry

More firms entering a market increases market supply (shifts right); firms exiting decreases it (shifts left). When profits in an industry are high, new firms are attracted in. When losses mount, firms exit. This entry/exit dynamic is a central mechanism in market economies.

2.5 Movement Along vs. Shift of the Supply Curve

The same logic applies to supply as to demand. This distinction is equally important — and equally tested:

  • A price change → movement along the supply curve = “change in quantity supplied.”
  • A non-price change → shift of the supply curve = “change in supply.”

2.6 From Individual Supply to Market Supply

Just as market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demand curves, market supply is the horizontal summation of all individual firms’ supply curves. At each price, you add up the quantities that every firm in the market is willing to supply. The resulting market supply curve is upward sloping and lies further to the right than any single firm’s supply curve.

3   Market Equilibrium

3.1 Bringing Supply and Demand Together

We have separately described what buyers want to purchase (demand) and what sellers want to sell (supply) at various prices. But markets involve both buyers and sellers. The central question is: at what price will the market actually settle?

The answer is equilibrium — the price at which the plans of buyers and sellers coincide.

Core Definition
Market Equilibrium

The condition in which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied: Qd = Qs. The price at which this occurs is the equilibrium price (P*), also called the market-clearing price. The corresponding quantity is the equilibrium quantity (Q*).

At the equilibrium price, there is no pressure for the price to change — every buyer who wants to buy at P* can find a seller, and every seller who wants to sell at P* can find a buyer. The market “clears.”

Figure 3.1 — Market Equilibrium: Sneaker Market
Supply and demand intersect at E. At P*=$60, quantity demanded (6,000) equals quantity supplied (6,000). This is the market-clearing equilibrium.

3.2 Stability: How Markets Reach Equilibrium

What is remarkable about markets is that equilibrium is self-correcting. If the price is temporarily above or below equilibrium, forces are automatically set in motion to push it back. There are two deviations to consider: shortages and surpluses.

3.3 Shortage: Price Below Equilibrium

Key Concept
Shortage (Excess Demand)

A shortage occurs when the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied (Qd > Qs). It arises when the price is set below the equilibrium price.

When there is a shortage, buyers who cannot get the good are willing to pay more. This competition among buyers bids the price upward. As price rises, quantity demanded falls (movement along D) and quantity supplied rises (movement along S) — until the shortage is eliminated at equilibrium.

Figure 3.2 — Shortage: Price Below Equilibrium (P = $40)
At P=$40, Qs=4,000 but Qd=8,000 → a shortage of 4,000 pairs. Competing buyers bid the price up toward P*=$60, eliminating the shortage.

3.4 Surplus: Price Above Equilibrium

Key Concept
Surplus (Excess Supply)

A surplus occurs when the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded (Qs > Qd). It arises when the price is set above the equilibrium price.

When there is a surplus, sellers find themselves with unsold inventory. To clear their stock, they discount prices. As price falls, quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls — until the surplus is eliminated at equilibrium.

Figure 3.3 — Surplus: Price Above Equilibrium (P = $80)
At P=$80, Qs=8,000 but Qd=4,000 → a surplus of 4,000 pairs. Sellers with unsold inventory cut prices, pushing the market toward P*=$60.
Self-Correcting Markets: The beauty of the price mechanism is that shortages and surpluses are self-eliminating. No central authority needs to decree the price. As long as prices are free to adjust, the market will find its own equilibrium — Adam Smith’s “invisible hand” at work.
Situation Condition Market Pressure Result
Shortage (excess demand) P < P* → Qd > Qs Buyers compete, bid price up Price ↑ toward P*
Equilibrium P = P* → Qd = Qs No pressure; market clears Stable
Surplus (excess supply) P > P* → Qs > Qd Sellers cut prices to clear stock Price ↓ toward P*

3.5 Comparative Statics: Predicting a New Equilibrium

The most powerful use of the supply-and-demand model is predicting what happens when something in the world changes. The analytical method is called comparative statics: we compare the old equilibrium to the new equilibrium after a shock, without worrying about the precise path of adjustment.

The steps are always the same:

  1. Identify which curve is affected (supply, demand, or both) and by what factor.
  2. Determine the direction of the shift (right = increase; left = decrease).
  3. Find the new intersection of D and S.
  4. Compare the new P* and Q* to the old ones.

In summer, strawberries are in season — warmer weather and abundant harvest dramatically increase the supply of strawberries. The demand for strawberries (consumer tastes for them) doesn’t change much. What happens?

🤔 Resolving the Apparent Paradox
Why Do Both Strawberries and Airline Tickets Sell More in Summer, but at Opposite Prices?

In summer, both strawberry sales and airline ticket sales increase. But strawberry prices fall while airline ticket prices rise. Does this violate the Law of Demand?

No! The key is which curve shifted. For strawberries, it is supply that shifts right (seasonal harvest) — and when supply increases, quantity rises but price falls (Case A above). For airline tickets, it is demand that shifts right (summer travel) — and when demand increases, both price and quantity rise (Case B above). The Law of Demand is not violated; you just need to identify which curve is doing the shifting.

  Case C: Both Curves Shift — Ambiguous Outcomes

The hardest scenarios involve simultaneous shifts in both supply and demand. In these cases, one variable (P or Q) is determinate — its direction is clear — while the other is indeterminate without knowing the relative magnitudes of the shifts.

Worked Example 3.1
Beer in Blacksburg: Both Curves Shift

You are a consultant advising a brewery. You’re told that in Blacksburg: (1) VT enrollment will increase from 35,000 to 40,000 students, and (2) the cost of barley (a key input) will increase significantly. What will happen to the price and quantity of beer?

Step 1 — Effect on Demand: More students = more buyers. Beer is a normal good for most students. → Demand shifts right (increases).

Step 2 — Effect on Supply: Higher barley costs = higher production costs. → Supply shifts left (decreases).

Step 3 — New Equilibrium:

  • Price (P*): Demand up pushes price up. Supply down also pushes price up. Both shifts act in the same direction on price. → P* definitely rises.
  • Quantity (Q*): Demand up pushes quantity up. Supply down pushes quantity down. These forces act in opposite directions. → Q* is ambiguous — depends on which shift is larger.
    • If demand increase dominates: Q* rises.
    • If supply decrease dominates: Q* falls.
    • If equal: Q* unchanged.

The correct economic answer: price definitely rises; quantity could go either way — we cannot say without more information.

Key Terms

Quantity Demanded
The amount of a good a household would buy at the current market price, in a given time period, if it could purchase all it wanted.
Law of Demand
As price rises, quantity demanded falls (and vice versa), all else equal. Demand curves slope downward.
Demand Schedule
A table showing the quantity demanded at each possible price; each row is a point on the demand curve.
Normal Good
A good for which demand increases when consumer income rises. Most goods are normal goods.
Inferior Good
A good for which demand decreases when consumer income rises (e.g., instant ramen, generic brands).
Substitutes
Goods that can replace each other. A higher price for one increases demand for the other (e.g., Coke and Pepsi).
Complements
Goods consumed together. A higher price for one decreases demand for the other (e.g., cars and gasoline).
Change in Quantity Demanded
A movement along the existing demand curve caused by a change in the good’s own price.
Change in Demand
A shift of the entire demand curve caused by a change in a non-price determinant.
Market Demand
The horizontal summation of all individual demand curves; total quantity demanded by all buyers at each price.
Supply
The amount of a good producers are willing and able to offer for sale at a given price during a given period.
Law of Supply
As price rises, quantity supplied rises (and vice versa), all else equal. Supply curves slope upward.
Change in Quantity Supplied
A movement along the supply curve caused by a change in the good’s own price.
Change in Supply
A shift of the supply curve caused by a change in a non-price factor (costs, technology, expectations, number of sellers).
Market Equilibrium
The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (Qd = Qs). Also called the market-clearing price.
Equilibrium Price (P*)
The price at which the market clears; no tendency for change exists at this price.
Shortage (Excess Demand)
Qd > Qs; occurs when price is below equilibrium. Creates upward pressure on price.
Surplus (Excess Supply)
Qs > Qd; occurs when price is above equilibrium. Creates downward pressure on price.
Comparative Statics
The method of comparing two equilibria (before and after a shock) to predict the direction of change in P* and Q*.
Ceteris Paribus
Latin for “all else equal”; the assumption that only one variable changes at a time when analyzing its effect.
