Learning Objectives

After working through this handout, you should be able to:

  1. Define quantity demanded and explain the three conditions embedded in that definition.
  2. State the Law of Demand and sketch a downward-sloping demand curve from a schedule.
  3. Explain why some goods show exceptions to the usual downward-sloping demand, including perfectly inelastic (vertical) demand, Giffen goods, and Veblen/luxury goods.
  4. Identify the key determinants of demand and predict which way the curve shifts when each changes.
  5. Classify a good as normal or inferior based on how demand responds to income changes.
  6. Identify whether two goods are substitutes or complements and trace the price-chain effect.
  7. Distinguish precisely between a change in quantity demanded (movement along) and a change in demand (shift of the curve).
  8. Explain how market demand is derived from individual demand curves by horizontal summation.

Every market has two sides: buyers and sellers. This handout focuses entirely on the buyers’ side — what determines how much of a good consumers want to purchase and how that desire responds to price and other conditions.

1   What Is Demand?

1.1 Quantity Demanded — A Precise Definition

In everyday speech, “demand” just means wanting something. Economists use the word far more carefully.

Core Definition
Quantity Demanded

The amount (number of units) of a product that a household would buy in a given time period if it could buy all it wanted at the current market price.

Three phrases in that definition carry precise meaning:

  • At the market price. Individual buyers are price-takers — the market sets the price and the buyer then decides how much to buy.
  • In a given time period. Quantity demanded is meaningless without a time window. “Five burgers” tells us nothing; “five burgers per week” is meaningful.
  • If it could buy all it wanted. Both willingness and ability to pay must be present. Wanting a Ferrari does not constitute demand unless you can also afford it.

The full demand for a good is the entire relationship between every possible price and the corresponding quantity demanded. We represent this with a demand schedule (table) and a demand curve (graph).

2   The Law of Demand

Law of Economics
The Law of Demand

There is a negative (inverse) relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded, ceteris paribus (all else equal). As price rises, quantity demanded falls; as price falls, quantity demanded rises. This produces a downward-sloping demand curve.

The Latin phrase ceteris paribus is crucial: when we say “as price rises, quantity demanded falls,” we mean holding everything else constant — income, prices of other goods, preferences, and so on.

2.2 Exceptions to the Law of Demand

The Law of Demand holds for the vast majority of goods. However, there are some notable exceptions worth knowing about.

(a) Perfectly Inelastic Demand — Essential Goods with No Substitutes

For some goods — especially life-saving medicines like insulin — consumers must purchase the good regardless of price. There is no substitute, and going without is not an option. The demand curve is vertical: quantity demanded stays the same no matter what happens to price.

(a) Vertical Demand (e.g., Insulin)

Price changes, quantity stays fixed.

(b) Backward-Bending Demand

Demand first follows the law, then bends back.

(b) Giffen Goods — The Subsistence Paradox

Named after 19th-century economist Robert Giffen, these are highly inferior goods that make up a large share of a poor household’s budget — think of a basic staple like bread or rice in a subsistence economy. When the price of bread rises, the household’s real purchasing power falls so sharply that it can no longer afford meat or better food. The only affordable way to maintain caloric intake is to buy more bread, even at the higher price. This produces a backward-bending demand curve: for a range of prices, quantity demanded rises as price rises. However, beyond a certain price, even this mechanism breaks down — the staple becomes so expensive that the household simply cannot afford enough of it, and quantity demanded begins to fall again. The demand curve bends back to the usual downward slope.

(c) Veblen / Luxury Goods — The Prestige Effect

Named after economist Thorstein Veblen, these are status goods — designer handbags, premium watches, exclusive club memberships — where a higher price signals prestige. A luxury bag priced at $200 may attract few buyers; the same bag at $2,000 may attract more, because the high price itself signals exclusivity. This also produces a backward-bending demand curve: for a range of prices, quantity demanded rises as price rises. But at some point, the price becomes so extreme that even wealthy consumers pull back — the prestige effect is outweighed by the sheer cost, and the demand curve returns to its normal downward slope.

Key takeaway: The Law of Demand holds for most goods. Exceptions arise with essential goods (vertical demand — quantity does not respond to price at all) and with Giffen goods (inferior staples) and Veblen goods (prestige luxuries), both of which can show a backward-bending demand curve where quantity demanded rises as price rises, for opposite reasons — necessity vs. status.

3   What Shifts the Demand Curve?

The demand curve is drawn holding everything constant except the good’s own price. When any other underlying factor changes, the entire curve shifts — meaning a different quantity is demanded at every price level. We will focus on several key determinants.

3.1 Income

Income affects how much people can afford. The direction of the effect depends on the type of good:

Normal Good (e.g., Steak)

Income ↑  →  Demand shifts right

Inferior Good (e.g., Instant Ramen)

Income ↑  →  Demand shifts left

Key Distinction
Normal Good vs. Inferior Good

Normal good: demand and income move in the same direction. Income rises → demand rises (steak, vacations, new cars).

Inferior good: demand and income move in opposite directions. Income rises → demand falls, as consumers switch to better alternatives (instant ramen, generic-brand cereal, bus rides). “Inferior” is an economic classification, not a quality judgment.

3.2 Wealth

Income is a flow — money received per period (your salary). Wealth is a stock — the accumulated value of assets (home equity, savings, portfolio). Higher wealth generally increases demand for normal goods, just as higher income does.

Real-World Connection
The Wealth Effect: Housing Prices and Consumer Spending

The U.S. housing wealth boom of the early 2000s — and the resulting consumption surge — is a dramatic historical example of the wealth effect. When home prices rise, homeowners feel wealthier and spend more (demand shifts right). When home prices crash, homeowners cut back spending sharply (demand shifts left). 3.3 Prices of Related Goods

The demand for a good is affected by the prices of other goods in two distinct ways:

(a) Substitutes — Goods That Replace Each Other

Substitutes serve similar needs and can replace one another: coffee and tea, Coke and Pepsi, butter and margarine. When the price of a substitute rises, consumers switch to the good in question — demand shifts right.

Figure 2 — Effect of a Substitute’s Price Rising: Coffee Market
Tea’s price rises → consumers switch to coffee → demand for coffee shifts rightward to D₂.

(b) Complements — Goods Consumed Together

Complements go hand-in-hand: hot dogs and charcoal for grilling, cars and gasoline, printers and ink. When the price of a complement rises, consumption of both goods falls — demand shifts left.

Figure 3 — Effect of a Complement’s Price Rising: Hot Dog Market
Charcoal’s price rises → less grilling → demand for hot dogs shifts leftward to D₀.

3.4 Tastes and Preferences

Consumer preferences are shaped by culture, advertising, social trends, and health information. A positive shift in preferences toward a good increases demand (shifts right); a negative shift decreases it (shifts left).

A classic example is the Hula Hoop craze. In 1958, over 25 million Hula Hoops were sold in the first few months, and over 100 million within the year — with no change in price or income. Pure enthusiasm drove demand dramatically rightward almost overnight. Advertisers try to recreate this: they aim to shift demand curves rightward by making goods seem more desirable. Public-health campaigns shift demand for cigarettes or sugary drinks leftward.

3.5 Future Expectations

Consumers act not just on current conditions but on what they expect to happen. Two types of expectations matter:

  • Expected future price: If consumers expect a good’s price to rise in the future, many will buy more today — shifting current demand right. Conversely, if they expect prices to fall, current demand shifts left. According to TrueCar data, many car buyers deliberately wait until August, when new model-year vehicles arrive and dealers offer incentives on remaining inventory — they are acting on expected future price declines.
  • Expected future income: A law student in their final year, confident of a high salary upon graduation, may increase spending now — even before earning more. Expected higher future income shifts current demand right.

3.6 Number of Buyers in the Market

More buyers in a market increases total market demand (shifts right); fewer buyers decreases it (shifts left). Population growth, new market entrants, or reduced trade barriers that admit foreign buyers all expand the pool of buyers.

Worked Example 1
Identifying Shift vs. Movement — Pizza Market

Each of the following events affects the market for pizza. For each, state: (a) does the demand curve shift or is there a movement along the existing curve? (b) which direction? (c) why?

  1. Pizza restaurants lower their prices.
  2. Consumer incomes increase (pizza is a normal good).
  3. The price of soda (a complement to pizza) increases.
  4. The price of submarine sandwiches (a substitute for pizza) increases.
  5. A nutrition study reports that pizza consumption reduces cholesterol.

1. Movement along the demand curve. Lower pizza price → quantity demanded increases. This is a change in quantity demanded, not a change in demand. The curve does not shift.

2. Shift rightward (increase in demand). Higher income + normal good → consumers want more pizza at every price. This is a change in demand.

3. Shift leftward (decrease in demand). Soda is a complement to pizza. Soda prices up → people buy less soda → and eat less pizza alongside it. Demand for pizza falls at every price.

4. Shift rightward (increase in demand). Sub sandwiches are a substitute for pizza. If subs become more expensive, consumers switch to pizza. Demand for pizza increases at every price.

5. Shift rightward (increase in demand). The study improves consumer tastes for pizza. Consumers now want more pizza at every price — a change in demand.

4   Movement Along vs. Shift of the Demand Curve

This is one of the most important — and most commonly tested — distinctions in introductory economics.

Critical Distinction
Movement Along vs. Shift

Change in the good’s own price → movement along the existing demand curve. Call this a “change in quantity demanded.” The curve does not move.

Change in any non-price determinant (income, related prices, tastes, expectations, number of buyers) → the entire curve shifts. Call this a “change in demand.” A new demand curve is drawn.

Jargon Rule: Always say “change in quantity demanded” when the good’s own price changes (movement along). Say “change in demand” when a non-price factor changes (shift). Writing “demand increased” to describe a price-induced change is a technical error.
Figure 4 — Movement Along the Curve vs. Shift of the Curve
Moving from A to B along D₁ is a change in quantity demanded (caused by a price change). Shifting from D₁ to D₂ or D₀ is a change in demand (caused by a non-price factor).
Worked Example 2
Reading a Demand Diagram: Movement or Shift?

The diagram below shows the market for electric vehicles (EVs). D₁ is the original demand curve. D₂ is the demand curve after the federal government announces a $7,500 tax credit for EV purchases. Points A, B, and C are marked.

(a) What does the movement from A to B represent? What caused it, and what is the precise economic term?

(b) What does the horizontal distance between A and C represent? What caused it?

(c) A classmate says “EV demand increased when prices fell from P₁ to P₂.” Identify the error in this statement.

(a) Moving from A to B is a change in quantity demanded — a movement along D₁. It was caused by a fall in the price of EVs from P₁ to P₂. Because only the good’s own price changed, the curve itself did not move. The correct term is “quantity demanded increased,” not “demand increased.”

(b) The distance from A to C represents the increase in demand (a rightward shift to D₂). At the same price P₁, consumers now want to buy the quantity at C rather than A, because a non-price factor changed: the $7,500 tax credit raised the appeal of EVs at every price level. This is a change in demand, not a change in quantity demanded.

(c) The error is terminology. A fall in the EV’s own price causes a movement along the existing demand curve — a change in quantity demanded, not a change in demand. The demand curve itself did not shift; the classmate should say “the quantity demanded of EVs increased.”

5   From Individual to Market Demand

Everything above describes one household’s demand. But markets have many buyers. Market demand is the total demand of all buyers in the market, and it is derived from individual demands through a process called horizontal summation.

The idea is straightforward: at each possible price, ask every buyer how much they would purchase, then add those quantities together. The sum is the market quantity demanded at that price. Repeat for every price, and you have the market demand schedule. Plot it, and you get the market demand curve.

For example, suppose a market has just two buyers. At a price of $10, Buyer A wants 3 units and Buyer B wants 5 units. The market quantity demanded at $10 is 3 + 5 = 8 units. At $5, if Buyer A wants 6 and Buyer B wants 9, market quantity demanded is 15. Do this at every price and connect the dots — the resulting market demand curve lies further to the right than any individual’s curve, because it always represents a larger total quantity. Crucially, the market demand curve still slopes downward — the Law of Demand continues to hold.

Why does “horizontal” summation matter? Because we are adding quantities (the horizontal axis), not prices. At a given price, we sum across buyers. We never add up prices.

Figure 5 — Deriving Market Demand by Horizontal Summation
Buyer A
At $10, A wants 3. At $5, A wants 6.
Buyer B
At $10, B wants 5. At $5, B wants 9.
Market (A + B)
At each price, add the quantities: 3+5 = 8 and 6+9 = 15.
All three panels use the same scale. At every price, market quantity demanded equals the sum of all individual quantities. The market demand curve (DM) lies to the right of each individual curve.

Key Terms

Quantity Demanded
The amount of a good a household would buy at the current market price, in a given time period, if it could buy all it wanted.
Demand
The entire relationship between price and quantity demanded, summarised in a demand schedule or demand curve.
Law of Demand
As the price of a good rises, quantity demanded falls, all else equal. Demand curves slope downward.
Ceteris Paribus
Latin for “all else equal” — the assumption that only one variable changes at a time when tracing the demand curve.
Normal Good
A good for which demand increases when income rises. Most goods are normal (steak, vacations, new cars).
Inferior Good
A good for which demand decreases when income rises. Consumers switch away from it as they become wealthier.
Substitutes
Goods that can replace each other. A price rise for one shifts demand for the other rightward (e.g., Coke & Pepsi, coffee & tea).
Complements
Goods consumed together. A price rise for one shifts demand for the other leftward (e.g., hot dogs & charcoal).
Change in Quantity Demanded
A movement along the existing demand curve, caused only by a change in the good’s own price.
Change in Demand
A shift of the entire demand curve, caused by a change in a non-price determinant.
Market Demand
The horizontal summation of all individual demand curves; total quantity demanded by all buyers at each price.
Giffen Good
A highly inferior good (subsistence staple) for which quantity demanded can rise as price rises, producing an upward-sloping demand curve for a range of low prices.
Veblen Good
A luxury/prestige good for which quantity demanded can rise as price rises (higher price signals status), producing an upward-sloping demand curve for a range of high prices.
Horizontal Summation
The method of deriving market demand by adding individual quantities demanded at each price level.
